Summary

Note 3 asked what the global market portfolio (GMP) earns and pays in hours of the world’s work (Nakanishi 2026d). This note asks the same of a household’s four claims: home, pension, portfolio and deposits. How many hours of work does each claim earn a year, and what does a working life end up holding? It covers housing in 16 countries, {{n:bs_n}} balance sheets and {{n:lf_n}} stylised lives.

Five results follow.

  1. Before taxes and costs, the home earned about as much as the world’s capital; after them, less. Per 1,000 world hours, 1976–2025, housing gained a net {{n:h_net_7625_r}} hours a year over the hour and the GMP {{n:g_net_7625_r}}; housing’s surplus is its net rent. With a 1-point property tax and buying and selling every ten years, housing’s 1976–2020 net falls from {{n:h_net_7620}} to {{n:rb_taxcost}}, against {{n:g_net_7620}} for the GMP.
  2. Note 3’s rent yields used wrong weights, now corrected. Its v1.4 printed {{n:old_1976_p}}, {{n:old_2000_p}} and {{n:old_2020_p}}% for 1976, 2000 and 2020; the corrected yields, now in note 3 v1.5, are {{n:rent_1976_p}}, {{n:rent_2000_p}} and {{n:rent_2020_p}}% (Section 4.4).
  3. Dwellings and land are the largest household asset; the pay-as-you-go pension claim is left off the balance sheet. In {{n:bs_year}}, dwellings and land were {{n:bs_dw}}% of gross household assets (GDP-weighted). The accrued pay-as-you-go claim was a median {{n:pg_med}}% of GDP in {{n:pg_n}} European countries in 2021, or {{n:pg_nw}}% of household net worth where known ({{n:pg_nw_n}} countries).
  4. The pension earns the growth of hours worked, or less. A claim indexed to the wage bill earns, in hours, the growth of total hours worked: {{n:pd_7620}} per 1,000 a year in 1976–2020, negative in Germany, Japan and Finland. A claim indexed to average pay earns the growth of hours per worker, {{n:pd_avh_7620}} per 1,000; over a life, a funded 60/40 claim ends {{n:lf_fp}} to {{n:pv_fp_avg}} times larger.
  5. Over a life, buying a home and renting while investing come out about even. Among {{n:d_n}} buyers, the owner’s median lead at 65 is {{n:d_omr}} years of final pay, and the owner wins in {{n:lf_beats_pct_b}}% of them. Across {{n:fg_n}} variants of the assumptions that share ranges from {{n:fg_lo}}% to {{n:fg_hi}}%.

What a life holds depends on what each claim is indexed to. On one basis (national hours, 16 countries, 1976–2020), housing earned {{n:cl_home_pct}}% a year over the hour and a wage-bill claim {{n:cl_payg_pct}}%. A claim indexed to work keeps pace with work by design, and little more.

1. The question

Note 3 asked three questions of a market: what a unit of capital generates in hours of work, what it pays out, and what it needs to stand still. This note asks the same questions of a household. A household does not hold the GMP alone. In most rich countries its largest asset is its home. Its largest claim is often a promise from the state to pay a pension that grows with the wage bill or with average pay, which does not appear on its balance sheet at all. Its portfolio and its deposits come after those.

Each of these claims has its own return in hours of work:

  • The portfolio earns its total return less the growth of the hour. Note 3 measured this for the GMP.
  • The home earns its net rent yield plus the growth of rents plus any change in the price paid per unit of rent, less the growth of the hour. Note 3 left this open.
  • A pay-as-you-go pension whose claims grow with the wage bill — the return a balanced system can pay (Samuelson 1958; Aaron 1966) — earns the growth of the wage bill. Measured in hours, the growth of pay per hour cancels, and what is left is the growth of the number of hours worked: demography, participation and working time. A claim revalued with average pay per worker earns instead the growth of hours per worker.
  • Deposits and bonds earn interest less the growth of the hour.

The pension point is the note’s main conceptual step. A claim indexed to the wage bill is the one asset that is denominated in hours by construction. Its return in hours is not a market outcome; it is the change in how much work is done.

The note proceeds in four steps. Section 4 measures the home as note 3 measured the GMP. Section 5 sets out the household balance sheet. Section 6 measures the pension. Section 7 follows stylised lives from age 25 to 65 that hold all of these together.

2. Definitions

The world hour. The CC-WH series of Nakanishi (2026b): nominal GDP per hour worked, chained at GDP weights across 43 countries, in US dollars. It is hard data to June 2025 and provisional after. It is used as in note 3: December to December, anchored to the panel’s dollars per hour in 2014 Q1.

The national hour. A country’s nominal GDP in local currency divided by its total hours worked (World Bank GDP over Penn World Table employment × average annual hours, from 1961; Macrohistory Database GDP over Penn World Table hours for 1951–1960). A return “in national hours” is a local-currency return less the growth of this series. It answers a resident’s question: does my claim command more of my own country’s work?

Hours generated, needed and net. As in note 3: capital worth 1,000 hours that returns TR (log) generates 1,000 × TR hours’ worth of currency in a year; 1,000 × g of those are needed to keep pace with an hour growing at g; the net gain is the difference. Every return in Sections 4–6 is in these units, hours a year per 1,000 hours of the claim. Ten hours per 1,000 is 1% a year (log).

Housing’s parts. The total return on housing is the net rent yield (cash) plus the price change. The price change is split into a valuation change, minus the change in the log of the net rent yield, and the rest, “price excluding valuation”, which is close to the growth of rents. Across countries, valuation is the GDP-weighted mean of each country’s change in log rent yield, not the change in the yield of the 16-country portfolio as a whole (Section 4.1).

Years of own work, years of own pay. Net worth per worker measured in national hours, divided by annual hours per worker, equals net worth divided by GDP. The workers, the hours and the currency cancel. So “years of a country’s own work” is simply the wealth-to-GDP ratio (checked to machine precision). In Section 7, “pay” is the national hour with hours per worker held fixed. Wealth divided by pay at 65 is therefore “years of own final pay”, and equals wealth in national hours up to a constant. It is not years of measured earnings.

Two pension indexations. A claim indexed to the wage bill grows with pay per hour times total hours worked; this is the return a pay-as-you-go system can sustain. Notional defined-contribution schemes credit accounts with wage-bill, average-income or GDP growth, depending on the country. A claim indexed to average pay grows with pay per worker, as many national revaluation rules do. In national hours the first earns the growth of total hours worked, the second the growth of hours per worker.

3. Data and construction

{{T1}}

Housing, to 2020. The Macrohistory Database, release 6 (Jordà, Schularick and Taylor 2017), gives annual housing total returns, capital gains and net rent yields for 16 countries (Jordà, Knoll, Kuvshinov, Schularick and Taylor 2019), built on the house price series of Knoll, Schularick and Steger (2017). Its net rent yield is rent less maintenance, management, insurance and depreciation, imputed for owners. The total return equals the capital gain plus the rent return to machine precision. The rent return equals this year’s rent yield times one plus the capital gain, i.e. rent over the start-of-year price, to machine precision; Australia 1950 is the one exception. Countries are weighted by start-of-year US-dollar GDP. The US weight averages {{n:h_usa_w}}% over 1976–2020. Returns in US dollars use the database’s exchange rate, xrusd, which is an end-of-year rate: Japan reads {{n:jpy85}} for 1985 and {{n:jpy86}} for 1986, the year-end rates, not the annual averages. December-to-December exchange rates therefore line up with the December-to-December world hour.

A unit problem in the database’s GDP. The database’s nominal GDP, gdp, is in millions, billions or trillions of local currency, depending on the country. Dividing it by the exchange rate does not give US dollars. Here each country’s series is rescaled by a power of ten fitted to World Bank US-dollar GDP; the largest residual standard deviation, in log10, is {{n:unit_resid}}. Note 3 did not do this (Section 4.4).

Housing, 2021–2025. The database ends in 2020. The OECD analytical house price indicators give a nominal house price index and a rent index for every one of the 16 countries. The price is chained onto the database’s 2020 level with the OECD index (annual average). The rent yield is the database’s 2020 yield times the growth of the rent index divided by the price index. Two gaps are filled: Australia’s 2025 rent from the first three quarters, and US 2025 rent from the BLS consumer-price index for rent of primary residence. Exchange rates after 2020 are BIS December averages, within {{n:fx_ext_gap}}% of the database’s 2020 rates. The OECD rent index measures rents paid by sitting tenants, not new leases; it lags market rents, so part of the 2021–2025 valuation change may be that lag.

Cross-check. Over 1976–2020, for each country, the database and the OECD indices agree closely: median correlations of annual growth are {{n:cc_p}} for prices, {{n:cc_pr}} for the price–rent ratio and {{n:cc_r}} for rents, and the median gap in price growth is {{n:cc_pg}} points a year. Substituting OECD rent growth for the database’s implied rent growth ({{n:cc_n}} country-years) moves the 1976–2020 world-hour net from {{n:h_net_7620}} to {{n:rb_oecd}} (Table A5).

Balance sheets. OECD Table 720 (financial assets and liabilities) and Table 9B (non-financial assets), for households and the non-profit institutions serving them, in national currency at year-end. Gross assets are financial assets plus all non-financial assets: dwellings, all land held by households, and other produced assets. “Dwellings + land” therefore includes all household land, not only the land under homes. The latest year with at least 90% of the full country count is {{n:bs_year}}. The US balance sheet from 1952 is the Federal Reserve’s Z.1 table B.101; OECD US net worth is {{n:oecd_z1}} times the Z.1 figure in 2023.

Pension entitlements. Eurostat’s Table 29 records the accrued-to-date value of pension promises, including the social-security schemes that the core accounts leave out, every three years (Eurostat and ECB 2020). These are present values under each country’s own discount rate and indexation assumptions, so levels differ for reasons other than the promises themselves.

Hours worked and population. Penn World Table 11.0 (Feenstra, Inklaar and Timmer 2015) gives persons engaged and average annual hours to 2023; their product is total hours. Sections 4 and 7 read them through FRED’s copies, which are identical to the PWT 11.0 file used in Section 6. For 2024–2025 (housing’s national hour only), total hours are extended with World Bank labour-force growth. The UN World Population Prospects 2024 gives the population aged 20–64, as estimates to 2023 and as the medium-variant projection after.

From note 3. The GMP’s monthly total return and cash yield, built on the published weight history (Nakanishi 2026c), and the world hour, are read unchanged from the earlier notes’ frozen files, whose checksums are listed in the Appendix.

4. The home in hours

4.1 Per 1,000 hours of housing capital

{{T2}}

Over 1976–2020, housing generated {{n:h_tot_7620}} hours a year per 1,000 hours of housing capital, against {{n:g_tot_7620}} for the GMP. The two totals are close. Their make-up is not. Housing paid {{n:h_cash_7620}} hours as net rent; the GMP paid {{n:g_cash_7620}} in cash. Valuation added {{n:h_val_7620}} hours to housing and {{n:g_val_7620}} to the GMP. Both needed {{n:h_need_7620}} hours to keep pace with the hour. So housing gained a net {{n:h_net_7620}} hours a year and the GMP {{n:g_net_7620}}; excluding valuation, {{n:h_netex_7620}} and {{n:g_netex_7620}}. Extended to 2025, the nets are {{n:h_net_7625}} and {{n:g_net_7625}}, and {{n:h_netex_7625}} and {{n:g_netex_7625}} excluding valuation.

How valuation is measured matters a little. Valuation here is the GDP-weighted mean of each country’s change in log rent yield. Measured instead as the change in the yield of the 16-country portfolio as a whole (same weights in both years), it is {{n:ex_val_port}} hours rather than {{n:ex_val_mean}}; the {{n:ex_val_gap}}-hour difference sits inside “price excluding valuation”, and net excluding valuation becomes {{n:ex_netex_port}} instead of {{n:h_netex_7620}}. Price excluding valuation is close to, but not equal to, the growth of rents: ln(1 + g + y) − ln(1 + y) ≠ ln(1 + g), where g is the capital gain and y the rent return. The mean gap over 1976–2020 is {{n:pxv_gap_mean}} points a year, and the largest is {{n:pxv_gap_max}}.

The periods differ more for the GMP than for housing. In 1976–1981, housing gained {{n:h_net_7681}} hours a year net while the GMP lost {{n:g_net_7681_abs}}. In 1982–2000 the GMP gained {{n:g_net_8200}} against housing’s {{n:h_net_8200}}. In 2001–2020 housing led again, {{n:h_net_0120}} to {{n:g_net_0120}}. In 2021–2025 housing gained {{n:h_net_2125}} and the GMP {{n:g_net_2125}}, but {{n:h_val_2125}} of housing’s hours were valuation: house prices outran rents. In the {{n:r20_n}} twenty-year windows within 1976–2020, housing beat the GMP in {{n:r20_win}}. Housing’s net ranged from {{n:r20_h_lo}} to {{n:r20_h_hi}} hours a year; the GMP’s from {{n:r20_g_lo}} to {{n:r20_g_hi}}.

Over 1976–2020, the annual net of housing had a standard deviation of {{n:h_sd}}%, against {{n:g_sd}}% for the GMP, and the two were uncorrelated ({{n:hg_corr}}). Much of that smoothness belongs to the index, not to a house. House-price indices are often annual averages and appraisal-based, and an index of many houses has none of the risk of one house.

From 1976 to 1999 the GMP’s cash yield was above the rent yield in {{n:gc_above_rent_7699}}% of years. From 2010 to 2020 the rent yield (between {{n:rent_1020_lo}} and {{n:rent_1020_hi}}%) was above the GMP’s cash yield (between {{n:gc_1020_lo}} and {{n:gc_1020_hi}}%). 6. The pension in hours

6.1 Why a claim on the wage bill earns the growth of hours worked

Samuelson (1958) and Aaron (1966) showed that a pay-as-you-go pension can pay each generation a return equal to the growth of the wage bill: the growth of pay per hour plus the growth of hours worked. Measure that claim in national hours, i.e. divide by GDP per hour, and the first part cancels if pay per hour grows with GDP per hour. What remains is the growth of total hours worked. If the labour share falls, pay per hour grows more slowly than GDP per hour, and the claim earns less than hours growth. This is the return the system can sustain, not the indexation rule of any one country; notional defined-contribution schemes credit accounts with wage-bill, average-income or GDP growth, depending on the country. Individual entitlements are usually revalued with average wages or with prices. A claim revalued with average pay per worker earns, in national hours, the growth of hours per worker, which was negative ({{n:pd_avh_7620}} per 1,000 a year over 1976–2020, Table 10). Over 1976–2019 the labour share of the 16 Macrohistory countries, weighted by purchasing-power GDP, fell from {{n:ls_a}}% to {{n:ls_b}}% (Penn World Table); a wage-bill claim would then have earned about {{n:ls_eff}} hours per 1,000 a year less than hours growth ({{n:ls_eff_usd}} with US-dollar weights) (Karabarbounis and Neiman 2014 document the fall). The identity holds in the stylised lives of Section 7 exactly: a 10% contribution builds a claim worth, in years of final pay, 10% times the sum of each year’s ratio of total hours at 65 to total hours in that year.

7. A life

7.1 Design

The life-cycle exercise follows {{n:lf_n}} stylised workers: one for each of the 16 Macrohistory countries and each cohort that starts work at 25 in a year from 1950 to 1980 and retires at 65, between 1990 and 2020 ({{n:lf_poss}} possible lives; Germany before {{n:deu_from}} and Japan before {{n:jpn_from}} lack the data; Table A1’s long-period column also needs national hours and the change in rent yield, so it starts later). Time steps are annual and flows come at the end of each year.

  • Pay is the national hour with hours per worker held fixed; before 1960 it is chained back with the database’s GDP per head. It is not measured earnings: where hours per worker fell, as in France, Japan and Sweden, actual annual pay grew more slowly. Because pay here is output per hour at fixed hours, not earnings, a home at 4 times pay or a 35% budget is a larger multiple of actual earnings than it looks: at the average labour share of {{n:ls_mean}}% (1951–2019, Penn World Table), about {{n:earn_home}} years and {{n:earn_budget}}% of earnings.
  • Budget. Each year {{n:lf_b}}% of pay goes to housing and saving; the rest is consumed and is the same for every rule.
  • The home costs {{n:lf_M}} times pay in the cohort’s first full year of pay (the year after it starts work at 25, age 26), and its price then follows the database’s nominal house price index. Gross rent is the net rent yield plus running costs of {{n:lf_c}}% of the price.
  • R (rent and invest) pays gross rent and saves the rest of the budget in a domestic 60/40 portfolio of the database’s equity and bond returns. In the GMP variant the account holds the domestic 60/40 until 1975 and the GMP, in US dollars converted to local currency at the database’s year-end exchange rate, from 1976.
  • O (own) rents and saves as R until 35, then buys if the account holds a {{n:lf_dep}}% deposit plus {{n:lf_buyc}}% buying costs (otherwise it waits). The mortgage is {{n:lf_ltv}}% of the price, a {{n:lf_term}}-year annuity at the long rate plus {{n:lf_spread}} point, fixed at purchase. O then pays the mortgage and running costs and saves the rest. Wealth at 65 is the home less any remaining loan plus the account.
  • Overdraft. Under both R and O the account may go below zero; a negative balance is charged the bill rate plus {{n:lf_ospread}} point.
  • The pension. On top of the budget, {{n:lf_tau}}% of pay goes either into a pay-as-you-go claim whose value grows with the national wage bill (nominal GDP, i.e. the wage bill at a constant labour share), into a claim revalued with average pay per worker, or into the same 60/40 account.
  • Valuation. O is re-run with the rent–price ratio frozen at its purchase-year level; the difference is the part of O’s wealth that came from valuation.

Wealth is reported in years of own final pay. The model checks itself: with all returns set to zero, R’s wealth equals the sum of its contributions; the funded claim equals 10% of summed pay; the pay-as-you-go claim equals the hours-growth formula of Section 6.1; and every decomposition adds up to machine precision.

Neighbouring cohorts share 39 of their 40 years, so the {{n:lf_n}} lives contain far fewer independent histories than their number suggests: in effect 16 countries and a few distinct periods. Shares of lives are descriptions of this history, not probabilities.

The design was corrected once, after the first results were seen. The design fixed before any result priced the same standard home for every cohort at 4 years of pay in 2000, and did not let the account go below zero. That home cost far more than 4 years of pay in the 1950s (Table 15), and most early renters could not pay the rent from their first working year. The baseline was therefore changed to a home costing 4 years of pay in each cohort’s first working year, and negative balances were allowed at the bill rate plus one point. The original design is kept as a robustness row (Table 16): its median wealth at 65 is {{n:a2_R}} (R) and {{n:a2_O}} (O) years of final pay, {{n:a2_negR}} renters go negative, and {{n:a2_never}} owners never buy. Because the baseline was chosen with the results in view, Section 7 is the least protected part of this note against the author’s choices.

{{T13}}

The median renter ends with {{n:lf_R}} years of final pay and the median owner with {{n:lf_O}} ({{n:lf_Os}} after selling costs). The median ratio of O to R is {{n:lf_OR}}, with an interquartile range of {{n:lf_OR25}}–{{n:lf_OR75}}; the median difference is {{n:lf_OmR}} years of final pay (interquartile range {{n:lf_OmR25}} to {{n:lf_OmR75}}; mean {{n:lf_OmR_mean}}). The median ratio of exactly 1.00 and difference of exactly 0.00 arise because the {{n:lf_never}} lives that never buy have O = R and sit at the middle of the distribution. Among the {{n:d_n}} buyers the median difference is {{n:d_omr}} (interquartile range {{n:d_omr25}} to {{n:d_omr75}}; Table 13), and the owner wins in {{n:lf_beats_pct_b}}%. The difference is the safer headline: where the renter’s wealth is near or below zero the ratio loses its meaning. The owner comes out ahead in {{n:lf_beats}} of {{n:lf_n}} lives ({{n:lf_beats_pct}}%). Among the {{n:lf_nn}} lives whose account never went below zero, the median ratio is {{n:lf_nn_OR}}, the median difference {{n:lf_nn_OmR}} and the owner wins in {{n:lf_nn_beats}}%. The median buyer buys at {{n:lf_buyage}}; {{n:lf_never}} lives never buy. In world hours, one hour of own final pay buys a median {{n:lf_q}} world hours; wealth in world hours divided by the fixed annual hours per worker is a median {{n:lf_Rw}} for R and {{n:lf_Ow}} for O.

{{T14}}

Why the two come out close. The means add up exactly. On average across the {{n:d_n}} buyers, the home’s price gain ({{n:d_pg_m}} years of final pay) plus the rent saved net of running costs ({{n:d_nr_m}}), less mortgage interest ({{n:d_int_m}}), buying costs ({{n:d_bc_m}}) and the 60/40 return forgone on the deposit and the extra outlays ({{n:d_fr_m}}), gives {{n:d_omr_m}}: {{n:d_pg_m}} + {{n:d_nr_m}} − {{n:d_int_m}} − {{n:d_bc_m}} − {{n:d_fr_m}} = {{n:d_omr_m}}. The last term also contains the difference in overdraft interest between O and R. The medians in Table 13 show the typical size of each item but do not add up. A home, bought with a mortgage, is a leveraged claim on housing; renting and investing is a claim on the 60/40. Over the 1950–2020 working lives their returns were close, so the result turns on the cost of the debt and on how well the 60/40 did.

By cohort. The median ratio of O to R was {{n:dc_50}} for cohorts starting in the 1950s, {{n:dc_60}} for the 1960s, {{n:dc_70}} for the 1970s and {{n:dc_80}} for the 1980 cohort. Table 14 measures where the decline comes from. Two changes each remove most of it: variable-rate mortgages remove {{n:dv_removed}}% (the ratio falls only from {{n:dv_variable_50}} to {{n:dv_variable_80}}), and bills as the saving vehicle remove all of it (the ratio stays between {{n:dv_bills_lo}} and {{n:dv_bills_hi}}). The decline therefore needs both: fixed-rate debt, which inflation eroded for early buyers and which locked later buyers into high rates, and the comparison with a strong 60/40 portfolio after 1980, which later renters held for most of their working lives. The two overlap and cannot be added. The US 1970 cohort is an example: it bought in {{n:us70_buy_year}}, at {{n:us70_age}}, at a mortgage rate of {{n:us70_mort}}%, paid {{n:us70_int}} years of final pay in interest, overdrew to {{n:us70_min}} years of pay, and ended with {{n:us70_O}} against the renter’s {{n:us70_R}}.

{{T16v}}

Valuation is a small part. Holding the rent–price ratio at its purchase-year level, valuation accounts for a median {{n:lf_val}}% of the owner’s wealth at 65 (interquartile range {{n:lf_val25}} to {{n:lf_val75}}%). The owner’s gain over a life came from net rent and from rent growth, not from a rising price per unit of rent.

Lives that overdraw. In all, {{n:lf_negany}} lives go below zero at some point: {{n:lf_negR}} under R and {{n:lf_negO}} under O, and every R case is also an O case. The median lowest balance among them is {{n:lf_negmin}} years of pay. By country (Table A4), the number of lives that overdraw under O is {{n:neg_top}}, and {{n:neg_rest}} or fewer elsewhere. In all, {{n:lf_endneg}} lives end below zero; all of them never buy, so O and R are identical for them. The French cohorts of {{n:fr_first}}–{{n:fr_last}} reach as low as {{n:fr_min}} years of pay: in the database, French house prices rose much faster than pay between 1951 and 1965, so a home worth 4 years of pay in 1951 was worth about {{n:fr_pp_65}} by 1965, with a net rent yield that put rent above a full year’s pay. The others are cohorts in {{n:neg_others}}. In Finland, Norway and Sweden the database’s rent yields are high relative to prices, so most lives there overdraw. The database’s post-war rent levels in these countries may be imperfectly measured. An overdraft costs the bill rate plus one point, which is usually below the mortgage rate, so these lives borrow cheaply and unsecured; that flatters both R and O where overdrafts are large.

7.3 The pension over a life

A contribution of 10% of pay for 40 years builds a pay-as-you-go claim worth a median {{n:lf_payg}} years of final pay if it is indexed to the wage bill and {{n:pv_avg}} if it is indexed to average pay per worker, and a funded claim worth {{n:lf_fund}} (a median {{n:pv_fp_avg}} times the average-pay claim). The average-pay figure depends on the framing: contributions here scale with pay at fixed hours while the claim is revalued with actual hours per worker; if contributions also fell with hours per worker, the claim would be exactly 10% × 40 = 4.00 years of final pay. The funded claim is a median {{n:lf_fp}} times the pay-as-you-go claim (interquartile range {{n:lf_fp25}}–{{n:lf_fp75}}) and is larger in {{n:lf_fp_win}}% of lives. That advantage is entirely the 60/40’s return over the growth of the wage bill. With the GMP as the vehicle from 1976 the ratio is {{n:ro_gmp_fp}}; with bills it is {{n:ro_bill_fp}}, and the funded claim wins in only {{n:ro_bill_fpwin}}% of lives. The pay-as-you-go claim is valued here at its indexation return, not at any country’s real benefit rules, and there is no survival risk.

The same home — an index of roughly constant quality — cost a median {{n:pp_1950}} years of pay in 1950, {{n:pp_1970}} in 1970, {{n:pp_2000}} in 2000 and {{n:pp_2020}} in 2020. 9. What it means, and what it does not

What it means. In hours of work, the home and the investable portfolio were both claims on capital, and both earned, before taxes and costs, {{n:cl_cap_lo}} to {{n:cl_cap_hi}}% a year more than the growth of the hour (1976–2025, world hours); after a property tax and transaction costs, the home earned less than the portfolio. They earned it differently: the home through rents that kept rising once valuation is set aside (price excluding valuation {{n:h_pxv_7625}} hours a year against the GMP’s {{n:g_pxv_7625}}), the portfolio with more help from falling yields (valuation {{n:g_val_7625}} against {{n:h_val_7625}}). A household that owned its home and a household that rented and invested ended a working life in much the same place, and which one came out ahead depended on the cost of its mortgage and on how well the 60/40 did. The large difference is between those claims and a pension claim indexed to the wage bill. That claim keeps pace with work by design, and beyond that it earns only the growth of the amount of work done. That growth was under 1% a year, was negative in Germany, Japan and Finland and about zero in France and Italy over 1976–2020, and the UN projection of the population aged 20–64 turns negative on GDP-weighted average after 2030. A claim revalued with average pay per worker did worse still.

What it does not mean. It is not advice to buy, rent or opt out of a pension. A state pension also insures against living long, against inflation and against market losses, none of which is valued here. The comparisons are of indices:

  • An index is not a house. There is no selection, no idiosyncratic risk, and no depreciation beyond the database’s cost deduction. The 16 countries are survivors, all now rich; their housing and equity returns are likely higher than those of a random sample of countries over the same years.
  • No taxes. No income, property, transfer or capital-gains tax, and no tax relief on mortgages or pensions, which in many countries favour both owning and funded saving. Imputed rent on an owner’s home is untaxed in most countries, which favours owning.
  • Index arithmetic. The balance-sheet return of Section 5.4 is a weighted average of index returns, not a measured household return.
  • Units. The wage-bill return equals hours growth only if pay per hour keeps pace with GDP per hour; the falling labour share cost about {{n:ls_eff_abs}} per 1,000 a year over 1976–2019 (Section 6.1). National and world hours answer different questions; Sections 4 and 6 compare like with like where they can.
  • Present values. The Table 29 entitlements depend on national discount rates, which differ by country; no common-rate version is available.
  • The life design was corrected after the first results were seen (Section 7.1); the database’s early rent levels in France and the Nordic countries drive the overdrafts; overdrafts are cheaper than mortgages.
  • Data ends. The database ends in 2020; 2021–2025 uses OECD indices and rents of sitting tenants; the world hour after mid-2025 and the Penn World Table after 2023 are not yet data.
